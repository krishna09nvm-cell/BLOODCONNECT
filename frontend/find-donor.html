    Universal donors, universal recipients & rare blood groups

    15 fictional profiles that explain how blood compatibility works. They are not real people, not registered donors and not patients, and they are never included in the nearby search.

    Read this first

    • Universal red-cell donor (O−): O− red cells have no A, B or RhD antigen, so they can be given in an emergency before the patient's group is known. This is about red cells only: O− plasma contains anti-A and anti-B, so it is not universal plasma (AB plasma is).
    • Universal red-cell recipient (AB+): AB+ blood has no anti-A or anti-B and is RhD positive, so in the ABO/Rh system it can receive red cells of any group. For plasma, an AB patient needs AB plasma.
    • "Universal" only covers the ABO and RhD antigens. Other antigens (such as Kell, Duffy and Kidd) and the patient's antibodies can still cause reactions, and platelets and plasma follow different rules.
    • Rare groups are never universal. A Bombay-phenotype (hh) person looks like group O in a routine test but can receive only Bombay blood. Rare groups need laboratory confirmation.
    • Real transfusion compatibility is always decided by a qualified blood bank using blood grouping, antibody screening and crossmatch tests.
